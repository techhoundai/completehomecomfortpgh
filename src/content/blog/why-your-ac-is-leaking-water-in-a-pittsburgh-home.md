## Why Your AC Is Leaking Water in a Pittsburgh Home

Water around an air conditioner gets people nervous fast, and for good reason. In a Pittsburgh summer, a little condensation is normal, but water on the floor, stains on the ceiling, or a drip coming from the air handler means something in the drain path is blocked, iced up, or broken.

The tricky part is that the leak is usually a symptom, not the real problem. If you just mop the floor and ignore it, the unit keeps making water, the cabinet keeps rusting, and the ceiling or drywall around it takes the hit.

## What the water is supposed to do

An air conditioner removes heat and humidity from the air. That moisture condenses on the evaporator coil, runs into a drain pan, and exits through a condensate line, or a pump if the equipment sits below the drain point.

When everything is set up right, you never think about that water. It disappears quietly, which is exactly how it should work.

### Normal condensation vs a real leak

A healthy system can produce a steady amount of condensate on a hot, humid day. That water should stay inside the drain system, not show up on the floor or in the ceiling below an attic unit.

If the water is pooling, dripping, or staining surrounding surfaces, the drain path is failing somewhere. And in older Pittsburgh homes, that failure can show up fast because a lot of equipment is tucked into tight attics, basements, closets, or additions with less-than-perfect drainage.

## The most common reasons an AC leaks water

### 1. The condensate drain line is clogged

This is the one we see all the time. Dust, algae, slime, and debris build up in the drain line, water backs up, and the pan overflows.

If the line is partially blocked, you might hear gurgling or see water around the indoor unit only when the system has been running for a while. If it’s fully blocked, the pan fills fast and the leak gets obvious.

### 2. The evaporator coil is frozen

A frozen coil turns into a water problem the moment it thaws. Ice forms because airflow is weak, the filter is plugged, the blower is struggling, or the refrigerant charge is off.

Here’s the pattern homeowners notice: the AC runs, airflow gets weaker, the supply air feels warm, then later you find a puddle when the ice melts. The water is real, but the freeze-up is the actual issue.

### 3. The drain pan is cracked, rusted, or out of position

Older pans don’t last forever. Metal pans rust through, plastic pans crack, and a sloppy installation can leave the pan pitched the wrong way so water never reaches the outlet cleanly.

That shows up a lot in older homes around Pittsburgh where equipment has been swapped over the years. One contractor changes the coil, another changes the furnace, and nobody gives the drain pan much attention until it leaks.

### 4. The condensate pump failed

If your AC sits in a basement or another low spot, a pump may be lifting water to a drain. When that pump fails, the water has nowhere to go.

Sometimes the pump motor dies. Sometimes the float switch sticks. Sometimes the tubing kinks. Either way, the symptom is the same, water where it should not be.

### 5. The system is producing more moisture than the drain can handle

Pittsburgh summers are humid, and a system that is oversized, short cycling, or running with poor airflow can build up moisture in weird ways. That doesn’t mean the AC itself is “making too much water,” it means the operating conditions are bad enough that the drain system gets overwhelmed or the coil starts to freeze.

Older homes make this worse. Leaky return ducts, undersized returns, and hot attic spaces all mess with how long the coil stays cold and how well water drains away.

## What Pittsburgh homes do differently

Pittsburgh has a lot of older housing stock, tight retrofits, basement installations, and attic air handlers that were never designed with perfect service access. That matters because water always finds the lowest point, and older homes usually have a few low points built in.

We also see more drain issues in homes with additions, finished attics, and long duct runs. When the equipment is squeezed into a space that wasn’t built for it, a small drain problem turns into ceiling stains or a basement puddle quicker than most people expect.

## What you can check safely before calling for service

You don’t need to tear the unit apart. A few simple checks tell you a lot.

1. **Turn the system off if water is actively leaking.** That stops a frozen coil from thawing all over the place and keeps the leak from getting worse.
2. **Check the air filter.** If it’s loaded with dust, replace it. Restricted airflow is a major reason coils freeze.
3. **Look at the drain line exit, if you can see it.** If you have a visible condensate line, see whether it looks blocked, disconnected, or dripping where it should not.
4. **Check for ice on the indoor coil or refrigerant line.** If you see ice, shut the system down and let it thaw before restarting.
5. **See whether the leak lines up with one side of the unit or a ceiling below it.** That helps figure out whether the drain pan, the pump, or the coil area is the source.

Do not poke around in wiring or try to clear the drain with random chemicals. A clogged drain can often be cleared the right way, but the wrong product can damage the line or the drain components.

## Why a leaking AC sometimes points to a bigger problem

A water leak is often the first clue that airflow or refrigerant conditions are off. If a filter is dirty, a return is undersized, or the blower wheel is filthy, the coil can get too cold and ice over.

If the refrigerant charge is wrong, you can get the same result. That’s why the leak matters, but the service call should focus on why the leak happened, not just where the water landed.

## When a leak becomes a repair-now problem

Call for service right away if you see any of these:

- Water dripping from a ceiling or wall cavity
- Ice on the indoor coil or copper lines
- A drain pan that fills repeatedly
- A pump that will not shut off or will not start
- Rust, corrosion, or mold around the air handler
- Repeated leaks after you already changed the filter

The longer water sits, the more expensive the fix gets. Drywall, insulation, flooring, and framing all hate condensate leaks, and they lose that fight every time.

## How to keep it from happening again

A good maintenance visit is what keeps most of these headaches from coming back. That means cleaning the drain line, checking the pan, confirming the float switch works, inspecting airflow, and making sure the coil is clean enough to breathe.

In a Pittsburgh home, that extra attention matters. Between humid summers, older ductwork, and equipment tucked into odd spaces, a small maintenance miss can turn into a soaked ceiling faster than people think.

If your AC is leaking water, don’t just keep a bucket under it and hope for the best. Find the cause, fix the drainage or airflow issue, and get the system back to doing its job, which is cooling the house without decorating the floor.
