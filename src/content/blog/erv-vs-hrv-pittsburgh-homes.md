Pittsburgh homes hold onto smells, moisture, and stale air faster than a lot of people expect. Tighten up an old house on the North Side, add insulation in a Mount Washington attic, swap in new windows in a Brookline rowhome, and suddenly the place stops breathing the way it used to. That is usually when homeowners start hearing about HRVs and ERVs.

Both systems bring in fresh air and push out stale air, but they do it in different ways. An HRV, or heat recovery ventilator, moves heat from outgoing air to incoming air. An ERV, or energy recovery ventilator, moves heat too, but it also transfers some moisture. In Pittsburgh, that moisture piece matters because we get cold, dry winters and sticky summers, and the wrong ventilation setup can make one season feel great while making the other one miserable.

## What does an HRV do?

An HRV uses two air streams that pass through a heat exchanger without mixing. Stale indoor air leaves the home, fresh outdoor air comes in, and the heat from the outgoing stream is transferred to the incoming stream during heating season.

That means you get outside air without throwing away as much conditioned air as you would with a simple exhaust fan. In winter, that helps keep incoming air from feeling like it came straight off the Monongahela in January.

### Where an HRV makes sense

An HRV is a good fit when winter moisture control matters more than summer humidity transfer. If a home already runs humid in summer, or if the homeowner wants the unit to avoid adding moisture back into the house, an HRV usually gets the nod.

It also fits houses that are pretty tight and need real mechanical ventilation, not just a bathroom fan that gets turned on after the shower fogs up the mirror. A lot of newer construction and well-sealed retrofits fall into that bucket.

## What does an ERV do?

An ERV works on the same general idea, but it lets some moisture move through the core along with heat. That gives you a little more humidity transfer between the outgoing and incoming air streams.

In winter, that can help keep indoor air from getting too dry. In summer, it can help reduce the amount of muggy outdoor moisture that gets pulled inside. For Pittsburgh, that is a real consideration because we do not just deal with cold. We deal with sticky, soup-bowl humidity too.

### Where an ERV makes sense

An ERV is usually the better pick when the home needs balanced ventilation through both heating and cooling season, and moisture management matters in both directions. If the house is tight and the AC is already fighting summer humidity, the ERV can soften the load a bit.

It is not a dehumidifier, and it is not a replacement for good AC sizing or proper drainage. But for a sealed-up home with real ventilation needs, it gives you fresh air without making humidity control harder than it has to be.

## ERV vs HRV, what is the actual difference?

The simplest way to think about it is this:

- **HRV:** transfers heat, not much moisture
- **ERV:** transfers heat and some moisture

That one difference is why the choice changes from house to house. If winter dryness is your main complaint, an ERV can help the indoor air stay a little less parched. If your house already gets damp and clammy, an HRV may be the cleaner choice.

The housing itself matters too. Pittsburgh has plenty of old masonry homes, additions, finished basements, and half-updated duct systems. A ventilation system that works beautifully in a new build in Cranberry might need a very different setup in a century-old home in Lawrenceville.

## Does Pittsburgh need mechanical ventilation at all?

A lot of older homes got away with leakage for ventilation. Air crept through cracks around windows, rim joists, attic bypasses, and old framing details. That was never good engineering, but it did move air.

Once you seal the house up, that accidental ventilation drops off. Then you start noticing things that were always there but never obvious, like lingering cooking odors, bathroom moisture, stale bedrooms, or that basement smell that hangs around long after the dehumidifier shuts off.

### Signs a home needs better ventilation

Look for these clues:

1. Condensation on windows in winter.
2. Cooking smells that stick around for hours.
3. Bathrooms that stay damp after the fan runs.
4. Stuffy bedrooms, especially on upper floors.
5. Persistent indoor humidity that does not track with the AC.
6. A home that smells fine in the morning and weird by evening.

If the house is tight and these problems keep showing up, mechanical ventilation is usually the right conversation. If the home is leaky as all get-out, sealing and insulation still matter, but you may not need as aggressive a ventilation strategy.

## Which one is better for old Pittsburgh homes?

That depends on how the house behaves. A leaky older home with no insulation upgrades may not need a full ERV or HRV right away, because the envelope itself is still moving a lot of air. But if the home has been tightened up, or if someone is planning a major remodel, that is when a ventilator starts making sense.

For many Pittsburgh homes, the decision comes down to moisture. If summer humidity is the bigger complaint, ERV often fits better. If winter indoor humidity is already on the high side, or if the house has moisture-heavy spaces like a basement laundry area or a lot of cooking load, HRV can be the safer play.

And if there are combustion appliances in the house, the design needs to be thought through carefully. A ventilation system should not create pressure problems that interfere with safe appliance operation. That is one of those details that separates a real install from a box that got hung on the wall and wished luck.

## What a proper ERV or HRV installation includes

A good install is more than setting a unit in a utility room and running a couple of ducts. The equipment needs balanced airflow, correct duct sizing, and controls that make sense for the house.

### The install should include:

- Dedicated supply and exhaust paths
- Balanced airflow measurements
- Insulated ducting where condensation is a risk
- Proper drainage if the unit produces condensate
- Filters that are easy to access and change
- Controls that let the homeowner run boost mode for showers, cooking, or parties
- Commissioning with actual airflow readings, not guesswork

In Pittsburgh basements, that duct insulation matters. Cold surfaces and humid air love to make water where you do not want it. If the unit or ductwork is installed poorly, the homeowner ends up paying for a ventilation system that brings in fresh air and a maintenance headache.

## Can an ERV or HRV lower humidity?

Not directly in the way a dehumidifier does. An HRV mainly exchanges heat, so it can help with winter ventilation without dragging as much moisture around. An ERV moves some moisture too, which can help moderate humidity swings.

But if the home has a serious summer humidity problem, the real fix is usually in AC sizing, airflow, duct performance, and sometimes a dedicated dehumidifier. Ventilation supports comfort. It does not cover for a system that is short on capacity or a basement that keeps feeding moisture into the house.

## How to choose between ERV and HRV

A simple rule of thumb helps get you started:

- Choose an **HRV** if winter moisture control is the bigger concern and you do not want moisture transferred back into the house.
- Choose an **ERV** if you want to manage both heat and moisture exchange through Pittsburgh’s heating and cooling seasons.
- Fix envelope leaks, attic bypasses, and basement moisture first, because no ventilator is a magic wand.

That last part matters. We see too many homes where somebody wants a ventilation system to solve what is really an insulation, air sealing, or humidity problem. Fresh air is good. Poorly controlled fresh air is just expensive outside air.

## What should homeowners ask before adding one?

Before buying anything, ask these questions:

1. Is the house tight enough to need mechanical ventilation?
2. Do we have a humidity problem, a stale air problem, or both?
3. Are there combustion appliances that need the pressure checked?
4. Where will the supply and exhaust ducts go?
5. How will the unit be serviced later?

If those questions do not have clear answers, the install is not ready yet. That is where a real HVAC evaluation pays off, because the right answer is based on the house, not just the equipment brochure.

Pittsburgh homes are a weird mix of old and new, leaky and sealed, damp and dry. That is exactly why ERV and HRV decisions should be made house by house. Get the ventilation right and the place feels cleaner, steadier, and easier to live in all year long.
