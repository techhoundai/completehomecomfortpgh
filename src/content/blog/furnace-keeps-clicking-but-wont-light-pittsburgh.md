A furnace that keeps clicking but never lights is usually stuck in the ignition sequence. The control board is trying to prove draft, fire the ignitor, open the gas valve, and confirm flame, but one step in that chain keeps failing.

In Pittsburgh, we see this a lot after a long summer idle, in damp basements, or on older furnaces with tired ignitors and crusty venting. The sound is the system trying, not the system succeeding.

## What the clicking noise actually means

Inside a modern furnace, that clicking is usually the control board or an ignition component trying to start combustion. A typical call for heat goes like this: thermostat sends the signal, the inducer motor starts, the pressure switch proves draft, the ignitor heats or sparks, the gas valve opens, and the flame sensor confirms the burners lit.

If you hear repeated clicking and never get flame, the furnace is failing one of those safety checks. That is by design, because a gas furnace should refuse to run if it cannot prove that ignition happened correctly.

### The furnace is not supposed to keep firing blindly

A lot of homeowners hear the clicks and assume the furnace is "trying hard." It is, but the board is also watching for proof that everything is safe.

If it cannot verify draft, ignition, or flame, it locks out and tries again. That protects the furnace, the house, and the people living in it.

## The most common reasons a furnace clicks but will not light

### A cracked or failed ignitor

On furnaces with a hot surface ignitor, that part glows red-hot to light the burners. It is brittle, and once it starts breaking down, it can fail intermittently, especially after the furnace has sat idle for months.

Spark ignition systems can fail too. The electrode, wire, or gap can be off just enough that you hear clicking but never get a clean spark at the burner.

### A dirty flame sensor

A dirty flame sensor usually shows up a little differently. The burners light, then shut off after a few seconds because the board never gets a good flame signal.

But it still belongs in the conversation, because a weak flame signal can look a lot like a failed ignition sequence to the person standing at the vent. If the furnace lights and then drops out, the sensor jumps way up the suspect list.

### Gas supply trouble

Sometimes the furnace is doing everything right and the problem is fuel delivery. A closed gas shutoff, a weak gas valve, low incoming gas pressure, a propane tank issue, or a problem in the gas line can all stop ignition.

In the Pittsburgh area, most homes run on natural gas, but plenty of surrounding homes still use propane or have long gas runs through additions and converted spaces. Long pipe runs and undersized lines can create headaches, especially if the furnace was changed out without the gas system being reviewed carefully.

### Pressure switch or venting problems

High-efficiency furnaces need proper draft through the inducer and vent pipes before they will light. If the pressure switch does not close, the board thinks the furnace cannot breathe safely and it will not open the gas valve.

That failure can come from a blocked intake, a blocked exhaust, a bad pressure switch, water in the tubing, a clogged condensate trap, or a bird nest in the vent. Pittsburgh winters, roof snow, and old exterior terminations can all add their own little brand of nonsense here.

### Control board or wiring faults

Sometimes the ignition parts are fine, but the signal never reaches them the way it should. Loose wiring, a weak transformer, a failed relay, or a control board issue can stop the sequence in its tracks.

Safety switches can also interrupt the call for heat. If the rollout switch, limit switch, or condensate float switch is open, the furnace may click, pause, and then lock out without lighting.

## What you can safely check before calling for service

A few checks are worth doing, and none of them require taking the furnace apart.

- Make sure the thermostat is set to **Heat** and the fan is on **Auto**.
- Check the air filter. A filter packed with dust can contribute to overheating and safety shutdowns.
- Look at the furnace door and any visible status light or error code.
- Make sure the gas shutoff valve near the furnace is parallel with the pipe, not turned sideways.
- Check the breaker once if the furnace is completely dead.
- If you smell gas, leave the house and call the gas company before anything else.

Do not keep hammering the reset button. One reset is enough. Repeated resets on a gas furnace are a good way to turn a small problem into a much worse one.

## Why Pittsburgh homes run into this problem more often

Pittsburgh homes have a few built-in quirks that make ignition problems show up more often. Basements stay damp, old utility rooms collect dust, and a lot of homes still have equipment tucked into tight spaces where air movement is poor.

That moisture and dust combo is rough on ignitors, electrical connectors, burners, and pressure switch tubing. Add long heating seasons, older venting, and furnaces that sit idle through the summer, and you get plenty of opportunities for small parts to fail right when the first cold snap hits.

High-efficiency furnaces can be especially picky. They rely on inducer draft, condensate drainage, and sealed venting, so if one of those pieces gets sloppy, the whole ignition sequence can get shut down before the burners ever catch.

## What a technician should test

A real diagnosis is more than listening to the clicking and swapping random parts. A good tech checks the whole ignition chain from the board to the burners.

- Ignitor resistance or spark output
- Flame sensor microamp signal
- Gas pressure at the manifold
- Pressure switch operation and vent draft
- Condensate drainage on 90 plus furnaces
- Rollout and limit switch status
- Burner condition, flame shape, and combustion quality

That test sequence matters because a failed ignitor and a gas supply problem can sound identical from the living room. The fix is different, so the diagnosis has to be different too.

If the furnace is locking out because the pressure switch is not proving draft, replacing the ignitor will not solve anything. Same deal if the board is fine and the gas valve is not opening because the incoming gas pressure is wrong.

## How to keep it from coming back

Most ignition problems do not start big. They start with a little corrosion, a dirty burner, a weak sensor, or a part that has been cycling for years.

A solid fall tune-up helps a lot. That means cleaning burners and the flame sensor, checking the ignitor, verifying draft, inspecting vent pipes, clearing the condensate drain on high-efficiency furnaces, and making sure airflow is not choking the system.

You can help on the homeowner side by changing filters on time, keeping the furnace area dry, and making sure returns are not clogged with furniture, boxes, or the random basement chaos that seems to live in every old Pittsburgh house.

## When to stop troubleshooting and call for repair

If the furnace clicks a few times and never lights, if it lights and shuts off right away, or if it locks out again after a reset, the problem needs a technician. The same goes for any gas smell, soot, yellow flames, or repeated error codes.

A furnace that will not light is not a nuisance to ignore. It is a system telling you something in the ignition chain is not right, and that is the time to handle it before a cold night turns into a no-heat call.

## FAQ

### Why does my furnace keep clicking but never start?

That usually means the ignition sequence is failing somewhere between draft, ignition, gas delivery, and flame proof. The furnace is trying to start, but a safety control is stopping it before the burners light.

### Can a dirty filter make a furnace fail to ignite?

Yes, indirectly. A badly clogged filter can restrict airflow, make the furnace overheat, and trip a safety limit switch before it ever completes the heating cycle. It is not the only cause, but it is one of the first things to check.

### Is it safe to keep resetting a furnace that will not light?

No. One reset is enough, then stop and find the cause. Repeated resets can mask the real problem and create a bigger safety risk if the furnace is trying to ignite without successfully proving flame.

### Why does this happen more with high-efficiency furnaces?

High-efficiency furnaces depend on the inducer, pressure switch, condensate drainage, and sealed venting before they will light. If any part of that chain is off, the furnace will lock out instead of running.

### Should I call for repair if the furnace lights and then shuts right back off?

Yes. That often points to a flame sensor, gas, airflow, or control problem. It is a different symptom from a furnace that never lights at all, but it still needs a proper diagnosis.
