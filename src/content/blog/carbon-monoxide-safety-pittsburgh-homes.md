Pittsburgh homes give carbon monoxide a few extra ways to cause trouble. Old basements, masonry chimneys, long heating seasons, and equipment that spends half the year working hard all add up to more chances for combustion problems to show up.

The bad part is that carbon monoxide is invisible and odorless. You do not smell it like natural gas, and you do not see it like smoke, so the only real defense is a combination of good equipment, decent venting, and properly placed CO alarms.

## What carbon monoxide is, and why HVAC systems are involved

Carbon monoxide, or CO, is a byproduct of incomplete combustion. Gas furnaces, boilers, water heaters, fireplaces, and generators can all make it if they do not burn fuel cleanly or if combustion gases do not leave the house the way they should.

A healthy heating system is supposed to take in air for combustion, burn fuel cleanly, and send the exhaust outdoors through a flue or vent. When the burner, heat exchanger, venting, or chimney has a problem, some of those combustion products can spill back into the home instead of leaving it.

### Furnaces and boilers are the usual suspects

In Pittsburgh, furnaces and boilers are the first places I look when someone reports a CO alarm or strange combustion behavior. Dirty burners, weak ignition, blocked venting, cracked heat exchangers, and flue draft problems can all push combustion out of range.

Older boilers in particular can be sneaky. They sit in damp basements, they vent through old masonry chimneys, and they often run for long stretches in January. That is a nice recipe for draft problems if the chimney liner, burner setup, or vent connector is not right.

### Water heaters and chimneys can join the party

A water heater can also make carbon monoxide if the burner does not burn cleanly or if the vent is blocked. A common mistake is to blame the furnace when the actual problem is a water heater with poor draft or a chimney shared by multiple appliances.

Chimneys matter a lot in older Pittsburgh homes. Clay liners crack, masonry absorbs moisture, and cold flues do not draft well at startup. If the chimney cannot carry exhaust up and out fast enough, those gases look for another path, and that path can be the house.

## What makes Pittsburgh homes more prone to CO problems

Pittsburgh has a lot of older housing stock, and that means a lot of equipment sitting in basements with long vent runs, patched chimneys, and air leaks that the original builders never had to think about. Add in modern exhaust fans, dryer vents, and tighter replacement windows, and the pressure balance in the house changes.

That pressure change matters. If a home pulls too much air out through a bath fan, range hood, or dryer, it can create negative pressure and backdraft combustion appliances. Instead of exhaust leaving the building, the room tries to steal air from the flue, and that can turn into a CO issue fast.

### Old masonry chimneys and cold basements

Cold chimneys draft poorly until they warm up. In a Pittsburgh winter, that means startup is the riskiest time for some boilers and furnaces, especially in basements that stay cold and damp.

If the flue stays too cool or the liner is compromised, exhaust can linger longer than it should. That does not guarantee a CO problem, but it does raise the odds that combustion gases are not moving the way they were designed to move.

### Tight winter homes and backdrafting

A house that has been air sealed and updated can still run into trouble if the combustion appliance is old. New windows, sealed attic bypasses, and stronger exhaust fans all change how air moves through the home.

That is why a heating system that used to behave fine can start causing trouble after a renovation. The equipment did not always get worse, the house just got better at starving it for air.

## What a CO alarm should do

A CO alarm is not there to tell you the furnace needs a new filter. It is there to warn you that CO has reached a level the alarm thinks is unsafe, based on how long the concentration has been present.

That means a low or brief spike might not set off the alarm right away, and a bigger or longer exposure will. You still treat every alarm seriously, because the sensor is trying to tell you something about the air in the house, not about the weather outside.

### Where to place alarms

Install carbon monoxide alarms on every level of the home, outside each sleeping area, and near attached garages. Follow the manufacturer instructions and local code, because placement rules can vary by device and jurisdiction.

Do not stick a CO alarm right next to a furnace, boiler, water heater, or above a cooking appliance. Those spots can give you nuisance readings or false confidence. The goal is to protect the people sleeping and living in the home, not to create a scoreboard near the equipment.

## Warning signs your combustion equipment needs attention

A CO alarm is the loudest warning, but there are other clues that a furnace or boiler is not burning or venting properly. None of these signs proves carbon monoxide on its own, but together they mean the system needs a close look.

Watch for:

- Soot around the appliance or vent connector
- A yellow or lazy burner flame instead of a steady blue flame
- Rust, corrosion, or water staining around the unit or venting
- Frequent pilot outages or ignition failures
- Moisture or staining near chimney connections
- Headaches, nausea, dizziness, or sleepiness that show up at home and ease up when you leave
- A furnace or boiler that struggles to start, short cycles, or seems to spill exhaust on startup

If you see those symptoms, do not just reset the thermostat and hope for the best. That is how people turn a small venting issue into a bigger one.

## What to do if the alarm sounds

If the CO alarm goes off, get people and pets outside first. Do not stand around sniffing equipment, and do not try to diagnose the problem in the basement while everyone else waits upstairs.

Once everyone is out, call 911 or the local fire department if anyone feels sick, weak, confused, or nauseated. If nobody feels ill and responders clear the home, the next step is a real HVAC and combustion inspection, because the alarm went off for a reason.

Do not keep resetting the alarm and running the furnace again and again. If the source is a venting or combustion problem, every restart gives the system another chance to spill gas into the house.

## What a technician checks during a CO call

A good CO diagnosis starts with combustion analysis, draft testing, and a look at the venting path. A tech is checking whether the appliance is burning cleanly and whether the exhaust is actually leaving the house the way it should.

That usually includes:

- Checking burner operation and flame quality
- Measuring combustion gases
- Inspecting the heat exchanger for damage or corrosion
- Testing draft on the appliance and chimney
- Looking for blocked vents, disconnected joints, or poor slope in vent piping
- Checking for backdrafting caused by exhaust fans or pressure problems
- Inspecting nearby water heaters, boilers, and other combustion appliances

Sometimes the furnace is the problem. Sometimes the water heater is the problem. And sometimes the house layout, the chimney, and the ventilation setup all work together to make a mess.

## How to lower the odds of a carbon monoxide problem

Start with annual maintenance on every fuel-burning appliance in the house. A clean burner, a solid ignition system, and a venting system that is still intact catch a lot of problems before they turn into a CO call.

Replace CO alarm batteries on schedule, test the alarms monthly, and replace the devices when the manufacturer says to. Most homeowners are good about smoke alarms and sloppy about CO alarms, which is backwards if you ask me.

If you have an older Pittsburgh home, pay extra attention after renovations, new windows, or ventilation changes. A house that gets tighter or more exhaust-heavy can expose a combustion problem that sat there quietly for years.

## Carbon monoxide safety is not a guessing game

If a furnace, boiler, or water heater is making the house smell odd, acting up on startup, or tripping a CO alarm, that is not a job for trial and error. The problem is either in the combustion process, the venting, or the pressure balance in the house, and those things need to be tested, not guessed at.

## FAQ

### What should I do first if my CO alarm sounds?

Get everyone, including pets, outside right away. If anyone feels sick, dizzy, or confused, call 911 or the fire department. Do not keep resetting the alarm and trying the furnace again until the home has been checked and cleared.

### Can a furnace make carbon monoxide without smelling like anything?

Yes. Carbon monoxide has no smell, which is why it is dangerous. You can have a combustion problem, a venting issue, or a cracked heat exchanger and not notice anything unusual until an alarm goes off or someone starts feeling sick.

### Do I need a CO alarm if I only have a boiler?

Yes. Any fuel-burning appliance can create carbon monoxide if something goes wrong, including boilers, furnaces, water heaters, fireplaces, and attached-garage vehicles. Put alarms on every level and near sleeping areas so you get warned early.

### Why are older Pittsburgh homes more likely to have CO issues?

Older homes often have masonry chimneys, basement equipment, long vent runs, and air leaks that change how combustion appliances draft. Add in winter humidity, sealed-up windows, and strong exhaust fans, and the house can start pulling air the wrong way through the venting system.

### Should I replace my furnace if a CO alarm goes off?

Not automatically. The alarm means the system needs to be checked, but the fix could be a venting issue, burner cleanup, airflow problem, or a failed part. If the heat exchanger is damaged or the appliance is unsafe to repair, replacement makes sense, but that decision should come after a proper diagnosis.
