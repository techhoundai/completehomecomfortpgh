Pittsburgh homes punish lazy furnace sizing. An old brick house in West View, a cape with patchwork insulation, and a 1920s place with a leaky attic do not all need the same furnace just because they’re the same square footage. And if somebody sizes equipment off a guess and calls it good, you usually find out the first cold snap when the house won’t stay even, the burner short cycles, or the utility bill starts acting like it’s mad at you.

A furnace that’s sized right doesn’t just make the house warm. It runs long enough to mix the air, keep the temperature steady, and handle Pittsburgh’s ugly little winter swings without acting nervous every five minutes.

## What does furnace sizing actually mean?

Furnace sizing means matching the furnace’s **delivered heat output** to the home’s actual heat loss. The number on the box is usually the input rating, measured in BTUs per hour, but what the house gets is the output after efficiency losses are accounted for.

If a furnace is rated at 100,000 BTU input and it’s 95 AFUE, the usable output is about 95,000 BTU per hour. That’s the number that matters for comfort. The wrong furnace can be too large, too small, or simply mismatched to the way the house breathes through the walls, attic, and ductwork.

## Why square-foot rules fail so often in Pittsburgh

Square footage is a lazy shortcut. It ignores insulation quality, window type, air leakage, ceiling height, exposure, duct losses, and whether the house was built when people thought caulk was optional. In Pittsburgh, those differences are huge.

A 1,600 square foot home in one neighborhood might have new windows, attic air sealing, and decent insulation. Another 1,600 square foot home might have original windows, a drafty rim joist, and a basement that leaks air like a screen door in January. Those two homes do not need the same furnace capacity.

### What a Manual J load calculation looks at

A Manual J calculation is the standard way to size heating and cooling equipment. It estimates the home’s heat loss based on actual conditions, not a rough rule or a contractor’s memory from the last job.

A proper load calculation looks at things like:

- Wall, attic, and floor insulation levels
- Window size, type, and orientation
- Air leakage and infiltration
- Ceiling height and house volume
- Duct losses in unconditioned spaces
- Local design temperatures and exposure

That’s the real math behind sizing. Not “this house looks like a 90,000 BTU house.”

## What size furnace do many Pittsburgh homes need?

There isn’t one magic number, because the house decides the size, not the zip code. But a lot of Pittsburgh homes end up smaller than people expect once the real heat loss is calculated.

We see plenty of homes that do fine with a furnace in the 60,000 to 80,000 BTU output range, and we also see older, leakier homes that need more. The point is not to guess high just to be safe. A furnace that’s too big can be just as bad as one that’s too small, and sometimes worse.

The output rating matters here. Two furnaces can both say 80,000 BTU input, but if one is 80 AFUE and the other is 96 AFUE, they deliver different amounts of usable heat. That changes the fit for the house.

## Why an oversized furnace causes real problems

Oversizing is the classic mistake. People think more capacity means more comfort, but furnaces are not pickup trucks. Bigger isn’t better if the house doesn’t need it.

When a furnace is too large, it heats the thermostat quickly and shuts off before the air has time to mix. That creates short cycling, more temperature swings, less even comfort, and more wear on ignition components, blowers, and controls. In a Pittsburgh house with cold bedrooms and a warm first floor, an oversized furnace often makes the imbalance feel worse because the system never runs long enough to smooth it out.

Oversized furnaces also spend less time in the steady part of the cycle where they do their best work. That means less consistent comfort and, often, less efficient real-world operation than the rating suggests.

## What happens when a furnace is undersized?

An undersized furnace runs and runs, especially on the coldest nights. The thermostat keeps calling for heat, but the house never catches up cleanly, so the system sits there grinding away.

You’ll see longer runtimes, lower supply-air temperature than expected during deep cold, and a house that feels fine in mild weather but struggles when the temperature drops. In Pittsburgh, that matters because our heating season is long enough for those weaknesses to show up. A furnace that just barely hangs on in November can become a nuisance in January.

Undersizing also exposes duct losses and airflow problems faster. If the equipment is already behind on capacity, leaky ducts and weak airflow steal even more of the heat the home needed in the first place.

## How ductwork changes the furnace size equation

Furnace sizing is not just about the box in the basement. Ductwork can steal capacity, choke airflow, or make a properly sized furnace behave like the wrong size.

Here’s what matters:

- **High static pressure** makes the blower work harder and can reduce airflow.
- **Leaky ducts** dump heat into a basement, attic, or crawlspace instead of the living space.
- **Small returns** starve the blower and reduce delivered heat.
- **Bad supply layout** can leave upstairs rooms cold and downstairs rooms overheated.

Pittsburgh homes have a lot of basements, additions, and old duct runs that were added in pieces over time. That means furnace sizing should always be tied to airflow checks and duct inspection, not just a BTU number on a sales sheet.

## How AFUE changes what furnace size you really need

AFUE tells you how much fuel energy becomes heat for the house. A higher AFUE furnace wastes less fuel, so the same input rating can deliver more usable heat.

That matters during replacement. If a homeowner is replacing an old 80 AFUE furnace with a 95 or 96 AFUE unit, the output changes even if the input size stays similar. You can’t just swap one-for-one without looking at the load calculation and the existing ductwork.

A higher AFUE furnace is not a license to oversize. It just changes the math. The home still needs a furnace that matches the actual heat loss.

## Signs a furnace is probably the wrong size

You don’t need a lab test to spot the warning signs. The house usually tells on the equipment.

Common signs of oversizing include:

- Short cycling
- Big temperature swings
- Hot blasts followed by long quiet spells
- Cold rooms that never seem to blend with the rest of the house
- More noise than you’d expect from a modern system

Common signs of undersizing include:

- Long, nearly nonstop runs in cold weather
- The house never fully recovering after setbacks
- Rooms feeling okay in mild weather but weak during cold snaps
- Thermostat settings that keep creeping higher just to stay comfortable

If you’re seeing a mix of these symptoms, the issue might be sizing plus ductwork, not one or the other.

## What should homeowners ask before replacing a furnace?

Ask the contractor how they calculated the load. If the answer is a shrug or a square-foot rule, that’s not a serious sizing process.

Good questions to ask:

1. Did you perform a Manual J load calculation?
2. What is the actual heat loss in BTUs per hour?
3. What output size does that point to, not just input?
4. What does the duct static pressure look like?
5. Will the blower setting be adjusted for the new furnace?
6. How does the new AFUE change the output compared with the old unit?

If those questions make somebody uncomfortable, that’s useful information.

## A better furnace size starts with the house, not the guess

The right furnace size comes from the home’s heat loss, the duct system, and the way the house actually behaves in a Pittsburgh winter. Old housing stock, leaky basements, attic insulation gaps, and ductwork that was never designed as a complete system all change the answer.
