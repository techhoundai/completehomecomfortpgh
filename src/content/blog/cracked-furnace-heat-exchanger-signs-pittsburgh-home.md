A furnace heat exchanger is the part that keeps combustion gases on one side and your indoor air on the other. When it starts to crack, the whole system gets dangerous in a hurry, because flue gas can mix with the air moving through your ducts. That is not a nuisance issue, it is a safety issue.

Pittsburgh homes put extra stress on furnaces for a simple reason, they run a lot. Long heating seasons, older equipment, and plenty of houses with basement installs, undersized returns, or ductwork that has seen better days all push a heat exchanger harder than it should be pushed. If the furnace has been overheating or running with bad airflow for years, the exchanger pays the price.

## What the furnace heat exchanger does

Inside a gas furnace, the burners heat the metal heat exchanger. The blower then pushes household air across the outside of that metal so the air picks up heat without ever touching the flame or the combustion gases.

That separation is the whole job. If the exchanger is intact, the furnace sends heat to the house and exhaust to the flue where it belongs. If the exchanger cracks, holes, or corrosion opens that barrier, combustion byproducts can move into the supply air stream.

## What are the warning signs of a cracked heat exchanger?

There is no single homeowner test that proves a crack. But there are signs that tell you the furnace needs a serious inspection, and fast.

### Soot, rust, or visible corrosion around the burners

Soot around the burner area, rust streaks on the cabinet, or corrosion near the burners and collector box tell you combustion is not staying as clean and controlled as it should. On condensing furnaces, rust and deterioration can also show up around the secondary heat exchanger or drain area if condensate has been sitting where it should not.

### A flame that looks lazy, lifting, or strange

A healthy gas burner flame should be steady and mostly blue. If the flame is lifting off the burner, fluttering, rolling out, or showing unusual yellowing, that points to combustion or airflow problems. It does not prove a cracked exchanger by itself, but it tells you the furnace is not operating cleanly.

### Burning odors, headaches, or carbon monoxide alarms

A furnace room that smells sharp, hot, or burned deserves attention. So does a carbon monoxide alarm that has gone off, even once. CO does not come from a cracked heat exchanger only, but a crack can absolutely put exhaust where it does not belong.

### Repeated overheating and shutdowns

If the furnace keeps tripping the limit switch, shutting down on high temperature, or running into short cycles under normal demand, the exchanger has probably been stressed by heat for a while. Overheating is one of the fastest ways to shorten exchanger life. Bad airflow, dirty filters, failed blower parts, and blocked returns are common causes.

### Unexplained soot inside the supply side

If a technician finds soot or combustion residue where only clean supply air should be, that gets attention immediately. A cracked exchanger can leak exhaust into the airstream, and the furnace can spread that contamination through the house every time the blower runs.

## Why Pittsburgh homes are rough on heat exchangers

Pittsburgh’s housing stock is full of older homes that were not built with modern forced-air systems in mind. Add a furnace retrofit, a few duct changes over the decades, and a return path that is less than ideal, and you have a recipe for higher static pressure and hotter heat exchangers.

That matters because heat exchangers fail from heat stress. A restricted filter, dirty evaporator coil, undersized return, blocked supply, or weak blower can raise furnace temperature well beyond the design range. The exchanger expands and contracts every cycle, and metal does not love that for 20 years straight.

### Basement installs and moisture

A lot of Pittsburgh furnaces live in damp basements. Moisture does not crack steel by itself, but it does help corrosion move faster, especially on older equipment with weak venting, condensate issues, or rust already starting at seams and welds.

### Long heating seasons

We do not get a furnace a few easy weeks of work and then call it a year. Pittsburgh furnaces see real mileage from October through April, and some years it feels like the season never really ends. More cycles, more expansion and contraction, more wear.

## What causes a heat exchanger to crack?

The usual suspects are pretty predictable.

1. **Overheating** from restricted airflow, dirty filters, or a weak blower.
2. **Age and metal fatigue** after years of expansion and contraction.
3. **Poor sizing**, especially furnaces that short cycle or run too hot because the system was not designed right.
4. **Corrosion** from moisture, venting problems, or chronic condensate issues.
5. **Delayed maintenance**, because small airflow and combustion problems turn into bigger problems when nobody catches them early.

A cracked exchanger usually is not a random event. It is the last chapter in a long story the furnace has been trying to tell somebody.

## How a technician checks for a cracked heat exchanger

This is where the job turns from guesswork into diagnosis. A good tech does not just eyeball the cabinet and declare victory or doom. They inspect the burners, the exchanger surfaces they can see, the flame pattern, temperature rise, venting, combustion readings, and the blower side of the system.

### Common inspection methods

- Visual inspection with mirrors, lights, or a borescope.
- Checking for soot, rust, and heat stress marks.
- Measuring temperature rise across the furnace.
- Looking at combustion and venting behavior.
- Verifying airflow issues that may have caused the problem in the first place.

Sometimes a crack is obvious. Sometimes it is hidden and only shows up under a camera or when the furnace is under load. And sometimes the furnace has symptoms that look scary, but the real problem is airflow or combustion setup rather than a failed exchanger.

## Can you keep running the furnace if you suspect a crack?

No, not if you have strong reason to suspect it. If the furnace is tripping a CO alarm, showing visible soot on the supply side, or making the house smell like exhaust, shut it down and call for service.

A cracked exchanger is not a “let’s see how it goes” problem. You are dealing with combustion gases, and those gases belong outside the occupied space. If the unit has a confirmed crack, it stays off until the equipment is repaired or replaced.

## Does a cracked heat exchanger always mean full furnace replacement?

Not always, but often. On some furnaces, replacing the exchanger is technically possible. In the real world, the labor can be expensive enough that replacement makes more sense, especially if the furnace is older, already inefficient, or has other worn parts waiting in line behind the exchanger.

Here is the practical way to think about it. If the furnace is newer and the rest of the system is in good shape, a repair can be the right move. If the furnace is older, noisy, inefficient, or has already been patched a few times, replacement usually puts you in a better spot long term.

## What homeowners can check before calling

You do not need to take the furnace apart. Stick to the safe stuff.

- Check whether the filter is dirty.
- Make sure all supply and return grilles are open.
- Look for water around the furnace.
- Confirm the carbon monoxide alarm is working.
- Note any smells, shutdowns, or unusual flame behavior.

If the furnace has a history of overheating, or you have already had airflow issues in the house, mention that when you call. Those details help a technician get to the root of the problem faster.
