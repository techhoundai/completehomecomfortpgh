A furnace pressure switch is one of those little parts nobody thinks about until the heat quits on a 24-degree Pittsburgh morning. It is a safety proving device, and if it does its job correctly, the furnace will not light unless the inducer fan creates the right draft first.

When that switch keeps tripping, the furnace is telling you something inside the venting, condensate, or inducer circuit is off. Sometimes the switch is bad. More often, it is just the messenger.

## What a furnace pressure switch actually does

The pressure switch verifies that the inducer fan is moving combustion gases the way it should. On a modern furnace, the control board starts the inducer first, then looks for a closed pressure switch before it allows ignition.

If the switch stays open, the furnace usually locks out. That prevents the burners from firing without proper draft, which is how you avoid flue gas spilling into the cabinet or the house.

### The basic sequence inside the furnace

1. Thermostat calls for heat.
2. The control board starts the inducer motor.
3. The inducer creates a pressure change in the combustion path.
4. The pressure switch closes.
5. The board opens the gas valve and starts ignition.

If any part of that chain breaks, the furnace stops right there.

## Why Pittsburgh homes trip pressure switches more often

Pittsburgh gives HVAC equipment plenty to complain about. We get wet snow, freeze-thaw swings, old housing stock, and a lot of retrofitted high-efficiency furnaces shoved into basements that were never designed with modern PVC venting in mind.

That matters because a pressure switch is only as happy as the venting system it is monitoring. Long vent runs, too many elbows, poor slope on condensate lines, and marginal inducer performance all show up fast in houses around here.

### 1. Intake or exhaust terminations are blocked

Snow, ice, leaves, nests, and even wind-driven debris can block a sidewall vent termination. In a Pittsburgh winter, a termination that worked fine in October can get buried after one sloppy storm or freeze-up.

High-efficiency furnaces need clear intake and exhaust paths. If the furnace cannot breathe, the pressure switch never sees the draft it is looking for.

### 2. Condensate is backing up where it should drain away

Condensing furnaces make water as part of normal operation. That water has to drain through the trap, tubing, and drain system without hesitation.

If the trap is clogged with sludge, the hose sags, or the drain line is partially blocked, water can interfere with the pressure switch tubing or the inducer housing. That is a very common winter problem in damp Pittsburgh basements.

### 3. The inducer fan is weak or not moving enough air

The inducer is the small blower that starts before ignition and pulls flue gas through the heat exchanger. If the motor is tired, the wheel is dirty, the gasket is leaking, or the housing is plugged with debris, the switch may never see enough pressure change to close.

This is one reason a bad pressure switch diagnosis gets botched. The switch is not the problem, the draft system is.

### 4. The pressure tubing has cracked, slipped off, or filled with water

Most furnaces use a small rubber or silicone tube to connect the inducer or collector box to the switch. If that tube cracks, gets pinched, or fills with condensate, the switch cannot read the pressure correctly.

A loose hose is cheap. The lockout it causes sure isn’t.

### 5. The vent design is marginal to begin with

We see this a lot in retrofit jobs. A furnace gets swapped into an old home, the vent run gets routed around framing or through a maze of joists, and nobody really checks the pressure rise across the system after startup.

Too many elbows, a run that is too long, poor pipe slope, or the wrong pipe size can create just enough resistance to make the switch chatter or fail to close consistently.

## What the error code is really telling you

Most furnaces will flash a code or LED pattern when the pressure switch does not close. That code does not always mean the switch itself failed. It means the furnace did not prove draft during the startup sequence.

If the furnace tries a few times and then locks out, the control board is protecting the equipment and the home. Repeated resets without fixing the cause usually just make the problem noisier.

## What a homeowner can safely check first

There are a few checks you can do without taking the furnace apart.

- Confirm the thermostat is actually calling for heat.
- Check the furnace filter, since airflow problems can create other faults that muddy the diagnosis.
- Look outside for snow, ice, leaves, or a visible blockage at the intake or exhaust terminations.
- Make sure the furnace switch and breaker are on.
- Look at the furnace’s error code if the panel is visible from the outside.

Do not jumper the pressure switch, and do not keep resetting the furnace over and over. If the switch is open for a real reason, bypassing it is a bad idea.

## What a technician should test

A proper diagnosis goes past “replace the switch and see what happens.” That kind of guessing burns time and money.

A good tech will check the pressure switch circuit with a meter, inspect the tubing, verify the inducer motor operation, and measure draft with a manometer. On a condensing furnace, they will also inspect the condensate trap, drain routing, collector box, and vent terminations.

### Measurements that matter

- **Pressure switch setpoint**, to see whether the switch is opening where it should.
- **Inducer vacuum or draft**, to verify the motor is pulling enough pressure.
- **Voltage to the inducer and switch circuit**, to rule out control board or wiring issues.
- **Condensate flow**, to make sure water is not backing up into the pressure circuit.
- **Combustion performance**, especially if the furnace has been struggling for a while.

If the furnace keeps tripping after those checks, the problem is usually the inducer, venting, condensate system, or a control issue, not the switch alone.

## When the pressure switch is the problem, and when it is not

Pressure switches do fail. The diaphragm gets weak, the contacts wear out, or the switch drifts out of spec after years of use.

But in the field, the switch is often doing its job. It opens because something else is wrong, and replacing it without finding the root cause just gives you a temporary victory lap.

### Common signs the switch itself is bad

- The furnace has proper draft, but the switch does not close.
- The switch tests inconsistently even when the inducer is running normally.
- The tubing, venting, and condensate system check out, but the furnace still locks out.

### Common signs the switch is only reporting a bigger problem

- The intake or exhaust is blocked.
- The inducer sounds weak, noisy, or slow to ramp up.
- Water is sitting in the condensate trap or pressure hose.
- The furnace only fails during very cold, windy, or snowy weather.

## Why this happens more in older Pittsburgh homes

A lot of Pittsburgh houses were built long before high-efficiency furnaces and PVC venting were on anyone’s mind. Basements are tight, utility rooms are crowded, and upgrades often happen in stages over decades.

That makes vent routing messier and service access worse. When a furnace is tucked into a corner with a long vent run and a condensate line that snakes across the floor joists, the pressure switch becomes the first thing to complain when something slips out of tune.

## How to keep the problem from coming back

Annual maintenance matters here more than people think. A tech can clean the inducer assembly, inspect the pressure tubing, flush the condensate trap, verify vent slope, and catch a weak motor before it starts causing lockouts on the coldest night of the month.

Homeowners can help by keeping the intake and exhaust clear, changing filters on schedule, and paying attention when the furnace starts acting flaky instead of pretending it will sort itself out. Furnaces are not shy about telling you when draft is getting marginal.

A pressure switch trip is usually a warning, not a mystery. Get the draft system, condensate path, and venting checked together, and you fix the actual problem instead of feeding the lockout machine.
