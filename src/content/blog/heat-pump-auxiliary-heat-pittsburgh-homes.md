Heat pumps do something a lot of Pittsburgh homeowners don’t expect, they call for auxiliary heat even when the outdoor unit is still running. That’s not automatically a failure. But if the backup heat is on all the time, or it’s kicking in on mild days, something in the system is out of balance.

In our part of western Pennsylvania, that extra heat shows up fast. Cold damp mornings, long recovery times after a thermostat setback, old houses with leaky ductwork, and a heat pump that’s a little undersized for the load all push the system toward backup heat. The trick is knowing what’s normal and what’s the system telling you.

## What auxiliary heat actually does

Auxiliary heat is backup heat that helps a heat pump when the compressor alone cannot keep up. In many residential systems, that backup is electric resistance heat strips inside the air handler. In a dual-fuel setup, the backup is a gas furnace, and the thermostat decides when the heat pump should step aside.

Heat strips are simple. Electricity runs through a heating element, the element gets hot, and the blower moves that heat into the ductwork. They are fast and dependable, but they are also expensive to run because they turn electricity directly into heat instead of moving heat the way the compressor does.

## Why your heat pump turns on aux heat

A heat pump does not use auxiliary heat just because it feels cold outside. It uses it when the indoor temperature is falling behind the thermostat setting, or when the unit is in a mode that needs help. That usually happens for a few predictable reasons.

### Big temperature setbacks

If you set the thermostat back a lot at night, the heat pump has to make up a large temperature swing in a short time. That is where aux heat shows up. The system sees a big recovery load and uses backup heat to catch the house up faster.

### Cold weather and the balance point

Every heat pump has a point where the outdoor unit can no longer carry the house by itself. That point is called the balance point. In Pittsburgh, that balance point depends on the home’s insulation, air leakage, duct losses, and the size and type of equipment, not just the weather app.

### Defrost mode

On damp winter mornings, frost builds up on the outdoor coil. The heat pump goes into defrost to melt that frost, and many systems bring on aux heat so the house does not feel cold while the outdoor coil is doing its thing. A little backup heat during defrost is normal. A system that lives in aux heat because defrost takes forever is not normal.

### Cold starts

When a heat pump first starts after the house has been sitting cold, auxiliary heat often joins in for a bit. That gives you warmer supply air sooner and helps the home recover. If it only runs briefly and then drops out, that is usually fine.

## Why Pittsburgh homes see more auxiliary heat use

Older Pittsburgh homes are tough on heat pumps. Plenty of them have drafty walls, older windows, tired attic insulation, and ductwork that leaks heat into basements and crawlspaces before the air ever reaches the bedrooms. A heat pump can do the job, but it has to work harder than it would in a tighter home.

River-valley humidity and damp winter air make the comfort picture even weirder. A house can feel cold and clammy at 34 degrees outside even when the thermostat says the indoor temperature is acceptable. The system senses the load, the indoor temp lags, and aux heat steps in more often than homeowners expect.

This is where bad sizing gets exposed. If the heat pump is too small, or the backup heat settings are too conservative, the auxiliary heat does too much of the heavy lifting. If the system is oversized on the cooling side and poorly matched on the heating side, it can create its own set of headaches. HVAC math matters here, not just brand names.

## When auxiliary heat is normal

A little auxiliary heat is part of normal heat pump operation. You should expect to see it:

- During a deep temperature recovery after a setback
- During defrost cycles on frosty, damp mornings
- On the coldest days of the season when the home’s heat loss rises
- For a short time after the system first starts up

If the house warms up, the backup drops out, and the outdoor unit keeps working, that is normal behavior. The system is doing its job.

## When auxiliary heat points to a problem

Aux heat that runs constantly is telling you the heat pump is not carrying its share of the load. In a Pittsburgh home, that usually comes down to one of a handful of issues.

### Thermostat setup is wrong

A thermostat can call for auxiliary heat too early if the staging settings are wrong, the balance point is set badly, or the wiring is not matched to the equipment. Some thermostats also treat recovery differently after a setback, which makes the system lean on backup heat more than it should.

### Airflow is restricted

Dirty filters, clogged coils, weak blower performance, or crushed duct runs cut the heat pump’s output. When airflow drops, so does delivered heat. The thermostat then keeps asking for more, and auxiliary heat keeps stepping in to cover the shortfall.

### Refrigerant charge is off

A heat pump with a low refrigerant charge does not move heat well. The outdoor unit can still run, but the compressor is working harder for less output. That turns into long runtimes, cold supply air, and extra auxiliary heat.

### Duct losses are high

Old ductwork in basements and crawlspaces leaks a lot of heat. The system may be producing heat at the air handler, but by the time it reaches the second floor, the supply air has lost too much temperature. Pittsburgh homes with long duct runs and uninsulated basements see this all the time.

### The heat pump is undersized for the house

A system that is too small will lean on auxiliary heat every time the outdoor temperature drops or the wind picks up. That is not a thermostat problem. It is a sizing problem.

## Emergency heat is not the same thing as auxiliary heat

A lot of homeowners mix these up. Auxiliary heat comes on automatically when the system decides it needs help. Emergency heat is usually a manual thermostat setting that shuts off the outdoor unit and runs backup heat only.

If you switch to emergency heat, you are telling the heat pump to sit on the bench. That setting is for a failed outdoor unit or a situation where the compressor should not run. It is not a normal everyday mode, and it can get expensive fast.

## What a technician checks first

When a heat pump uses auxiliary heat too often, we start with the basics and work outward. No guessing, no replacing parts because somebody had a hunch.

A solid diagnostic usually includes:

1. Checking thermostat settings and staging logic
2. Looking at outdoor unit operation, defrost behavior, and discharge temperature
3. Measuring airflow and checking the filter, coil, blower, and duct pressure
4. Verifying refrigerant charge and system pressures
5. Looking for duct leakage, insulation gaps, or obvious heat loss in the home
6. Confirming the auxiliary heat strips or backup furnace are not stuck on

That combination tells you whether the problem is control logic, airflow, refrigerant, equipment sizing, or home heat loss. A tech who skips those checks is just swapping parts and hoping for the best.

## How to keep aux heat from running too much

A few practical steps go a long way.

- Keep the filter clean, because restricted airflow makes the system lean on backup heat
- Avoid huge overnight setbacks, especially during cold snaps
- Make sure supply and return vents are open and unobstructed
- Seal obvious duct leaks in basements, attics, and crawlspaces
- Have the system checked before winter so staging, charge, and airflow are set correctly

And if your heat pump uses auxiliary heat on a 40-degree day in November, that is not something to shrug off. Something is making the system work harder than it should.
