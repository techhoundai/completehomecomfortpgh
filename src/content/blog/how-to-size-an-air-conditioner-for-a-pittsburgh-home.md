Pittsburgh is rough on cooling loads. A brick house in Bloomfield with old windows and a hot attic does not need the same air conditioner as a newer house in Cranberry with tighter ducts and better insulation, even if the square footage is identical. Size the unit by guesswork and you get the classic complaint, the downstairs is cold, the upstairs stays sticky, and the system shuts off before it ever dries the air.

We've seen too many homeowners get talked into a bigger unit because bigger sounds safer. Bigger often means shorter run times, weaker humidity control, and more wear on the equipment. In a humid Pittsburgh summer, that is a bad trade.

## What AC size actually means

A ton of cooling equals 12,000 BTU per hour. That number tells you capacity, not cabinet size, and it definitely does not tell you whether the system fits your house. The right size is the amount of cooling your home needs on a hot design day after you account for heat gain through the roof, walls, windows, ducts, and all the little air leaks that old houses love to hide.

That is why a small ranch in the South Hills and a three story brick house on the North Side can land on very different equipment sizes. Square footage is part of the picture, but it is not the picture.

### Manual J is the real starting point

Manual J is the residential load calculation contractors use to figure out heating and cooling needs. It looks at more than floor area. Insulation, window type, orientation, ceiling height, air leakage, occupancy, and internal heat from appliances all change the result.

For Pittsburgh homes, that matters a lot. A century old house with plaster walls and drafty trim does not behave like a newer build in the suburbs. If a contractor skips the load calculation and just matches the old unit, they're guessing.

## Why Pittsburgh homes are so easy to mis size

Western Pennsylvania housing stock is all over the map. You get brick colonials, row houses, bungalows, additions from three different decades, finished attics, enclosed porches, and basements that were never meant to be part of the conditioned space. Every one of those changes the cooling load.

Then there is the weather. Pittsburgh summers bring humidity, and humidity is where bad sizing really shows itself. An air conditioner is not just a cold air machine, it is a dehumidifier too. If the system short cycles, the thermostat might be satisfied fast, but the air still feels sticky.

A few things that commonly change the right AC size in Pittsburgh homes:

- Older windows with weak solar control
- Attics that get hammered by afternoon sun
- Additions tied into old ductwork
- Leaky rim joists and basement air leakage
- Long duct runs through hot attic spaces
- Second floors that sit right under the roof deck

Two homes can look almost identical from the sidewalk and still need very different cooling capacity. That is why square footage rules of thumb miss so often.

### Why square footage alone lies

A 1,600 square foot ranch and a 1,600 square foot two story home do not have the same heat gain. The ranch has more roof area, the two story may have a different window load, and the duct layout may be completely different. Orientation matters too, because a west facing wall takes a beating from the afternoon sun.

In other words, two houses with the same number on the tax card can have different tonnage needs by a half ton or more. Sometimes the gap is even bigger.

## What happens when the AC is too big

Oversizing is the classic mistake. On paper, a bigger unit sounds like extra comfort. In real life, it often makes the house feel worse.

A large system cools the air fast and shuts off before the coil has time to pull enough moisture out of it. That leaves you with short cycles, wide temperature swings, and a house that feels cool but damp. Pittsburgh homeowners notice this most on muggy evenings, when the thermostat looks happy and the rooms still feel clammy.

Oversizing also beats up the equipment. Starts and stops are hard on compressors, contactors, blower motors, and controls. The system does more work for less comfort, which is about as backwards as it gets.

## What happens when the AC is too small

An undersized system has the opposite problem. It runs and runs on hot days and still struggles to catch up. The house never quite settles down, and on the hottest afternoons you feel like the AC is always behind.

That said, an undersized system that is only a little off can still perform better than an oversized one if the ductwork is solid and the load is close. The goal is not to buy the biggest machine you can fit in the yard. The goal is to match capacity to the home.

## What matters besides tonnage

Tonnage gets all the attention, but it is only one piece of the system. If the airflow is wrong, the static pressure is high, or the ductwork is undersized, even the perfectly sized AC will underperform.

Here are the numbers and checks that matter just as much:

- **SEER2** tells you seasonal efficiency, not whether the unit is the right size.
- **Airflow** has to match the equipment. A rough target is about 400 cubic feet per minute per ton.
- **Static pressure** shows how hard the blower has to work to move air through the ducts.
- **Refrigerant charge** has to match the coil and outdoor unit.
- **Return air size** matters because a starved return chokes the whole system.

You can put a high efficiency system on bad ducts and still get poor results. Efficiency ratings do not fix bad design.

### Why airflow and ductwork matter so much

A lot of Pittsburgh homes have duct systems that were added later, patched over time, or squeezed into spaces that were never ideal. That is common in older neighborhoods where the house changed long before the HVAC system did. If the supply side is too small, the return is weak, or the ducts leak into a hot attic, the equipment cannot deliver the cooling it was built to deliver.

A proper sizing visit should look at the duct system, not just the outdoor unit. If the duct system cannot move the air, the new AC will not save the day.

## When zoning or a mini split makes more sense

Sometimes the problem is not the whole house. It is one stubborn second floor, a finished attic, a sun baked bonus room, or an addition that never got the airflow it deserves. In those cases, simply installing a bigger central AC is the wrong move.

Zoning or a ductless mini split can solve the comfort problem without overcooling the rest of the house. That is especially useful in Pittsburgh homes with tricky layouts, limited return air, or long duct runs through hot spaces. If one part of the house always runs hot, add control where the problem is instead of trying to brute force it with more tonnage.

## What a proper sizing visit should include

A real sizing visit is more than a five minute look at the old nameplate. It should dig into the home itself and how the air moves through it.

1. **Room by room load calculation.** This shows which spaces need the most cooling and where the hot spots are.
2. **Inspection of insulation and windows.** These are major load drivers in older Pittsburgh homes.
3. **Duct evaluation.** Supply size, return size, leakage, and static pressure all affect performance.
4. **Humidity discussion.** If the home feels muggy now, the new system has to address that.
5. **Equipment selection matched to the load.** The right capacity, coil, and blower setup matter.

If nobody asks about the attic, the windows, the ductwork, or the second floor, they are not sizing the system. They are selling equipment.

## How to think about replacement if your old AC worked "fine"

A lot of homeowners say the old system was fine until it died, so they assume the replacement should be the same size. That is not a safe assumption. The old unit could have been wrong from day one and the family just got used to living with it.

Homes also change. Maybe you insulated the attic, replaced windows, finished a basement, or added a bedroom. Any of those changes can shift the cooling load. If the house changed, the tonnage should be checked again.

## FAQ

### What size AC does my Pittsburgh home need?

There is no fixed size that works for every Pittsburgh home. The right answer comes from a Manual J load calculation that looks at the actual house, not just the square footage. Two homes with the same floor area can need different tonnage because of windows, insulation, attic space, and duct layout.

### Can I replace my old AC with the same size unit?

Only if the old unit was correctly sized and the house has not changed. If the old system was too big, too small, or the home now has better insulation or new windows, the same size replacement is not the best choice. A load calculation gives you a clean answer instead of a guess.

### Why does an oversized AC make my house feel sticky?

An oversized unit cools the air too fast and shuts off before it removes enough moisture. That leaves the temperature close to setpoint but the relative humidity too high, which feels clammy. In Pittsburgh's humid summers, that problem shows up fast.

### How long should my AC run?

A properly sized system should run long enough to cool the home and pull moisture out of the air without constant short cycling. If it is running in very short bursts or nonstop, something is off with the sizing, airflow, or ductwork. The system should settle the whole house, not just hit the thermostat and quit.

### Is SEER2 the same as AC size?

No. SEER2 is an efficiency rating, while size is the cooling capacity of the system. You can have a very efficient unit that is still the wrong tonnage for the house.
